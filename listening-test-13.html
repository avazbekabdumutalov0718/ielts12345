Section One. You will hear two friends called John and Anna talking about a holiday Anna has just had on Jackson Island.

John: Hi Anna. How was your holiday to Jackson Island?

Anna: It was good. There's quite a lot to see. It's quite a big island really.

John: Yeah. I was thinking of going in the summer. So where would you recommend going on the island?

Anna: Well the capital of course, Camford. I stayed there with my cousin. What did you do there? Well actually I spent most of my time there shopping. So I did all my shopping in the market, which is great. Just make sure you've got cash with you as most of the stalls don't take credit cards.

Okay, that's good to know. Where else did you go? Well my cousin and I went to Newtown. She said it was famous for its modern architecture. We only had half a day there so we took a bus tour around the town. But if I were you I'd give the bus tour a miss. There's a museum though, that's supposed to be good. You might like that.

Then after that we headed for Golden Beach. But we had to stay in a caravan as the hotels were all booked up. We did some sailing on the most beautiful blue sea. I'd suggest staying there for a minimum of four days if I were you.

From Golden Beach we drove into the centre of the island to White Mountain. We stopped in a motel instead. We tried snowboarding and it was harder than it looked. I wish I'd had some lessons.

One place you should try to visit is this very quaint cafe which is at the foot of the mountain. It has the best cakes ever. You'd be much better off in a car. It would be better to get a map here before you go.

Section 3. You will hear two trainee teachers called Dean and Hannah talking about their recent teaching practice in an elementary school.

Dean / Hannah: The main aim was getting the class to think about how people in different parts of the world are connected to each other through the things they buy and sell (A – global interdependency). And we also wanted to get the class thinking about how things are moved around the world in different ways (D – transport systems).

Problems we identified: the way we paced things. Some things seemed to be rushed and others seemed to drag out too long (E – the timing). And organizing the children in sets of six didn't work very well and that pairs would have been better (B – the student grouping).

Lesson plan: we got volunteers to mark the location of those producers (G) on a map of the world. Then we brainstormed the advantages and disadvantages of different methods of transport (H). They had to decide an itinerary for sending their raw materials to the USA – their export routes (A). After the break they filled in details on a worksheet (E). Then a class discussion about the future (B) of pencils. And finally the kids had to plan a short talk / presentation (C).

Section 4. You will hear a lecturer talking to a group of environmental science students about fuel made from biomass.

Using briquettes has a financial benefit because there isn't any cost involved for materials. Briquettes are less trouble to store.

At the initial stage all the loose plant material is ground into a powder. The machine forces the biomass mixture out through rectangular or circular holes. The amount of electricity / energy required is only 3% to 9% of the energy produced.

The second method: the biomass is made into a paste by adding water and something sticky such as starch.

In Europe and the USA, mainly for agriculture. In China in 2002 the number of factories producing biomass briquettes had increased to approximately 600. In Uganda most customers are public institutions or businesses.

In general the demand for biomass fuels is almost certain to grow.
