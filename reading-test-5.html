The lost animals of Australia

The history of Australia's animals over the past 50,000 years has been largely one of extinction. The time has been too short for new species of large animals to evolve and, over this period, nearly one-third of Australia's mammal species have become extinct, along with nearly all the large reptiles and many of the flightless birds. The cause of these extinctions is still hotly debated. One school of thought suggests that humans caused the extinctions, and another that they were due to changes in climate. The greatest problem in evaluating these theories is that we do not know exactly when these animals became extinct. Fossils from a number of well-dated cave and lakeside sites suggest that all the now-extinct species had already vanished 35,000 years ago, but a few other sites suggest that giant marsupials might have survived until 25,000 years ago, or even as late as 6,000 years ago.

One certainty is that Australia's climate has changed greatly over the past 40,000 years. Between 25,000 and 15,000 years ago, the sea level dropped by more than 100 metres and the continent experienced an extremely arid phase. Clearly, the timing of the extinctions is of critical importance. If they can be shown to coincide with the arrival of humans, about 50,000 years ago, then circumstantial evidence against human impact becomes much stronger. Conversely, if the extinctions can be shown to have occurred about 20,000 years ago, during the height of the last ice age, then climate change becomes the more likely cause.

A major breakthrough in the debate was the discovery of a deposit of fossilised bones at Cuddie Springs, a dried-up lake in north-western New South Wales. These fossils are particularly important because they are found in association with stone tools, showing that humans and giant animals co-existed at the site. Excavations at Cuddie Springs have yielded a wealth of giant animal fossils, including the diprotodon, a massive herbivore the size of a rhinoceros, and the genyornis, a huge flightless bird. Crucially, radiocarbon and other dating methods suggest that these fossils are about 30,000 years old, placing them well after the arrival of humans but before the height of the last ice age.

However, the Cuddie Springs findings have not been accepted by all scientists. Some critics argue that the site is not as straightforward as it seems. They suggest that the fossils and stone tools may have been mixed together by water action or geological processes, making the dating unreliable. Others argue that Cuddie Springs is a unique site and does not reflect the broader pattern of extinctions across the continent. They point to other evidence, such as fossilised eggshells of genyornis from dozens of sites across Australia, which consistently date to about 45,000 years ago, suggesting a rapid extinction shortly after human arrival.

To resolve the debate, scientists have turned to new techniques for dating fossils directly. One such technique is electron spin resonance (ESR), which measures the radiation damage in fossil teeth. By applying ESR and another dating method, uranium-series dating, to fossil teeth from Cuddie Springs and other sites, researchers have been able to obtain more precise ages. These studies suggest that some giant animals did indeed survive until about 30,000 years ago. However, the debate remains unresolved, with proponents of both the human-impact and climate-change theories finding evidence to support their views.

Even if climate change was the primary trigger for the extinctions, human activity may have played a significant role. For example, humans may have altered the landscape through fire-stick farming - the deliberate burning of vegetation to promote new growth and attract game. This practice could have dramatically changed the vegetation patterns of the continent, leading to the decline of species that depended on specific habitats. Furthermore, even if humans did not hunt giant animals to extinction, their presence could have placed additional stress on populations already struggling to adapt to a changing climate.
